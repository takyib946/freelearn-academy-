🎨 Visual Literacy Notes

Lesson 1: Meaning and Importance

What is visual literacy?

Visual literacy is the ability to understand, interpret, evaluate and communicate information presented through images, symbols, diagrams, charts, photographs and other visual forms.

Importance of visual literacy

  • Helps people understand information quickly.
  • Supports learning through diagrams, pictures and charts.
  • Helps people evaluate advertisements and media messages.
  • Improves communication when words alone are insufficient.
  • Encourages critical thinking about images and their meanings.

Example

A red traffic light communicates "stop" without needing a written sentence. A graph can show changes in student performance over time.

Lesson 2: Elements and Principles of Design

Elements of visual design

  • Line: Guides the viewer's eyes and outlines shapes.
  • Shape: A defined two-dimensional area.
  • Colour: Creates mood, contrast and emphasis.
  • Texture: Suggests how a surface feels or looks.
  • Space: The area around or between objects.
  • Form: Gives objects a three-dimensional appearance.
  • Value: The lightness or darkness of a colour.

Principles of design

  • Balance: Distribution of visual weight.
  • Contrast: Differences that make elements stand out.
  • Emphasis: The main point that attracts attention.
  • Unity: How elements work together as a whole.
  • Proportion: The relative sizes of elements.
  • Repetition: Reusing elements to create consistency.
  • Movement: Guiding the viewer's eyes through a design.
Lesson 3: Analysing Images and Visual Messages

When analysing an image, ask these questions:

  1. Observe: What objects, people, colours and symbols are visible?
  2. Describe: What is happening in the image?
  3. Interpret: What message might the creator be communicating?
  4. Evaluate: Is the image convincing, fair and reliable?
  5. Context: Who created it, for whom and for what purpose?

Example: An educational poster

A poster showing students reading books may promote education. Its audience could be schoolchildren. Bright colours and large lettering may attract attention. However, you should still examine the source and the message before trusting it.

Revision questions

  1. Define visual literacy.
  2. State four elements of visual design.
  3. Explain contrast and emphasis.
  4. Give two questions you should ask when analysing an image.

📐 Calculus Notes

Lesson 1: Functions, Domain, Codomain and Range

What is a function?

A function is a rule that assigns exactly one output to each permitted input.

Example: f(x) = 3x + 2
f(4) = 3(4) + 2 = 14

Domain

The domain is the set of all permitted input values. For f(x) = 3x + 2, the domain is all real numbers.

Codomain

The codomain is the stated set in which outputs are expected to lie. It is part of how a function is defined.

Range

The range is the set of outputs the function actually produces. For f(x) = x² over all real inputs, the range is y ≥ 0.

Useful domain rules

  • For a fraction, the denominator cannot equal zero.
  • For a real square root, the expression inside must be at least zero.
  • For a real logarithm, its argument must be greater than zero.
Lesson 2: Logarithms and Surds

Logarithms

A logarithm tells us the power to which a base must be raised to produce a number.

If 2³ = 8, then log₂(8) = 3.

Useful rules, for valid positive arguments:

  • logₐ(xy) = logₐ(x) + logₐ(y)
  • logₐ(x/y) = logₐ(x) − logₐ(y)
  • logₐ(xⁿ) = n logₐ(x)

Surds

A surd is an expression involving a root that cannot be simplified to a rational number.

√49 = 7
√12 = √(4 × 3) = 2√3
√89 cannot be simplified further using a perfect-square factor.

Quadratic equations and the discriminant

For ax² + bx + c = 0, where a is not zero:

x = (-b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a
Discriminant: D = b² − 4ac
  • D greater than zero: two distinct real roots.
  • D equals zero: one repeated real root.
  • D less than zero: no real roots.
Lesson 3: Differentiation and Integration

Differentiation

Differentiation measures the rate at which a function changes. It can also represent the slope of a curve at a point.

If f(x) = xⁿ, then f'(x) = nxⁿ⁻¹.
Example: if f(x) = x³, then f'(x) = 3x².

Integration

Integration can calculate accumulated quantities and areas. Indefinite integration also reverses differentiation, up to a constant.

∫xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹/(n + 1) + C, for n ≠ -1.
Example: ∫x² dx = x³/3 + C.

Revision questions

  1. If f(x) = 3x + 2, calculate f(4).
  2. State the domain of f(x) = 1/x.
  3. Simplify √12.
  4. Find the discriminant of x² − 5x + 6 = 0.
  5. Differentiate f(x) = x³.

🏫 Foundations of Education Notes

Lesson 1: Meaning, Aims and Functions of Education

Meaning of education

Education is the process through which people acquire knowledge, skills, values, attitudes and understanding. It can take place in schools, homes, workplaces and communities.

Aims of education

  • Develop knowledge and intellectual ability.
  • Build practical and professional skills.
  • Promote moral values and responsible citizenship.
  • Prepare people for employment and further learning.
  • Encourage creativity, problem-solving and social development.

Functions of education

  • Social: Passes culture and shared values to new generations.
  • Economic: Develops skills needed for work and productivity.
  • Political: Helps people understand citizenship and civic responsibilities.
  • Personal: Supports individual growth and fulfilment.
  • Cultural: Preserves and develops cultural knowledge and practices.
Lesson 2: Types of Education

Formal education

Structured learning in recognised institutions, usually following a curriculum, timetable and assessment system. Example: studying at a university.

Informal education

Learning that occurs through everyday life, experience, observation and interaction. Example: learning a recipe from a family member.

Non-formal education

Organised learning outside the usual formal school system. Example: a community workshop or a short vocational course.

Philosophy and education

  • Idealism: Emphasises ideas, values and the development of the mind.
  • Realism: Emphasises reality and learning about the world.
  • Pragmatism: Emphasises experience, practical activity and problem-solving.
  • Existentialism: Emphasises individual choice, responsibility and personal meaning.
Lesson 3: Teaching, Learning and the Teacher's Role

Teaching

Teaching involves planning and guiding learning experiences so that learners can develop understanding and skills.

Learning

Learning is a process through which knowledge, skills, attitudes or behaviour develop through study and experience.

Roles of a teacher

  • Plan lessons and communicate learning objectives.
  • Explain concepts and organise meaningful activities.
  • Assess progress and provide feedback.
  • Support learners with different needs.
  • Maintain a safe, respectful learning environment.
  • Model professional conduct and ethical behaviour.

Education and society

Education influences social mobility, economic development, citizenship and cultural change. Society also influences what is taught, how schools operate and who has access to educational opportunities.

Revision questions

  1. Define education.
  2. Explain three functions of education.
  3. Distinguish formal, informal and non-formal education.
  4. State four roles of a teacher.

📝 Technical Report and Communication Notes

Lesson 1: Communication and Its Types

Meaning of communication

Communication is the process of sending and receiving information, ideas or feelings between people.

Types of communication

  • Verbal: Spoken communication, such as discussions.
  • Written: Emails, reports, letters and messages.
  • Non-verbal: Gestures, facial expressions and posture.
  • Visual: Charts, diagrams, signs and pictures.

Communication process

A sender develops a message, sends it through a channel, and a receiver interprets it. Feedback helps show whether the message was understood. Noise or distractions can interfere with the process.

Barriers to communication

  • Language differences.
  • Noise and poor internet connections.
  • Unclear or overly complicated messages.
  • Cultural differences and assumptions.
  • Poor listening or lack of feedback.
Lesson 2: Technical Report Writing

What is a technical report?

A technical report is a structured document that presents information about a technical subject, investigation, project, procedure or findings.

Common report structure

  1. Title page: Report title, author and date.
  2. Abstract or executive summary: A brief overview, if required.
  3. Table of contents: Sections and page numbers, if needed.
  4. Introduction: Background, purpose and objectives.
  5. Methodology: How the work or investigation was conducted.
  6. Results or findings: What was discovered.
  7. Discussion: What the findings mean.
  8. Conclusion: The main outcomes.
  9. Recommendations: Suggested actions, where appropriate.
  10. References and appendices: Sources and supporting material.

The exact structure depends on your lecturer's instructions and the type of report.

Lesson 3: Academic Writing, Referencing and Emails

Features of good academic writing

  • Clear and precise language.
  • Logical organisation and paragraphs.
  • Evidence to support important claims.
  • Correct grammar, spelling and punctuation.
  • Appropriate formal tone.

Referencing and plagiarism

Referencing acknowledges the sources used in your work. Plagiarism includes presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own without proper acknowledgement. Follow the citation style required by your lecturer.

Professional email structure

  1. Use a clear subject line.
  2. Begin with a respectful greeting.
  3. State your purpose clearly.
  4. Provide relevant details.
  5. End politely and include your name.

Example email

Subject: Request for Clarification on Assignment

Dear Lecturer,

I hope you are doing well. I am writing to ask for clarification about the requirements for the assignment. Kindly let me know if there are any specific guidelines we should follow.

Thank you for your time and assistance.

Yours sincerely,
[Your Name]

Revision questions

  1. Define communication.
  2. State four barriers to effective communication.
  3. List five sections of a technical report.
  4. Explain why referencing is important.
